Semaglutide & Wegovy

What is semaglutide and how is it used for weight loss?

Quick Answer

Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist — a medication that mimics the gut hormone GLP-1 to reduce appetite, slow gastric emptying, and lower body weight by addressing the biological drivers of hunger directly. For weight management, it is available as Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4mg), which the FDA approved in June 2021 for chronic weight management in adults with a BMI ≥30, or BMI ≥27 with at least one weight-related comorbidity. In the STEP 1 trial, participants using semaglutide 2.4mg lost an average of 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks — a result that exceeds any previously available non-surgical intervention.

Key Takeaways

  • Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4mg) is FDA-approved for chronic weight management; Ozempic (semaglutide 0.5–2mg) is FDA-approved for type 2 diabetes — weight loss with Ozempic is an established off-label use, not an approved indication.
  • Semaglutide works by activating GLP-1 receptors in the hypothalamus and gastrointestinal tract, reducing appetite signals and slowing gastric emptying — it addresses the biology of obesity, not just behavior.
  • The STEP 1 trial (Wilding et al., NEJM 2021) demonstrated an average 14.9% reduction in body weight with semaglutide 2.4mg over 68 weeks in adults without diabetes.
  • Physician supervision is essential: dose titration over 16–20 weeks, baseline lab work, and ongoing monitoring determine whether semaglutide is working safely and effectively for each patient.
  • Semaglutide is contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN2) — a prescribing physician must review your full medical history before starting treatment.

What Semaglutide Is — and What It Is Not

Semaglutide is a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, a class of medication originally developed to treat type 2 diabetes. It is a synthetic analog of human GLP-1, a hormone naturally secreted by intestinal L-cells in response to food intake. Because semaglutide binds GLP-1 receptors with high affinity and has a half-life of approximately one week, a single weekly subcutaneous injection maintains consistent receptor activation — unlike endogenous GLP-1, which is cleared within minutes.

It is important to be precise about which semaglutide product is being discussed, because the same active molecule appears in two different FDA-approved drugs with different approved indications:

  • Ozempic (semaglutide 0.5mg, 1mg, 2mg) — FDA-approved in December 2017 for glycemic control in type 2 diabetes. Weight loss is a well-documented effect, but it is an off-label use of this formulation.
  • Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4mg) — FDA-approved in June 2021 specifically for chronic weight management in adults with BMI ≥30, or BMI ≥27 with at least one weight-related health condition such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, or dyslipidemia.

Patients frequently ask about "Ozempic for weight loss." Ozempic produces meaningful weight reduction, but it is not approved for that purpose. Wegovy is the FDA-approved option for weight management specifically, and it delivers the 2.4mg maintenance dose that produced the outcomes in the STEP clinical trial program.

How Semaglutide Works: The Biology of Appetite Regulation

Obesity is not a failure of motivation. It is a chronic metabolic disease in which the body's hunger and energy-storage systems are dysregulated. After significant weight loss, appetite-suppressing hormones fall and appetite-stimulating hormones rise — the body actively defends a higher weight set point. This is why repeated diet attempts produce diminishing returns for most patients with obesity.

Semaglutide intervenes at the biological level through several mechanisms:

  • Hypothalamic appetite suppression: GLP-1 receptors in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus regulate energy intake. Semaglutide activates these receptors, reducing the subjective experience of hunger and increasing satiety after smaller meals.
  • Slowed gastric emptying: Semaglutide delays the rate at which food leaves the stomach, extending the sensation of fullness after eating.
  • Reduced food reward signaling: Emerging data suggest GLP-1 receptor activation also modulates dopaminergic reward pathways, which may reduce cravings for calorie-dense foods.
  • Improved insulin secretion and glucagon suppression: In a glucose-dependent manner, semaglutide improves pancreatic beta-cell response — relevant for patients who also have insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes.

The result is a sustained reduction in caloric intake that is driven by altered physiology, not willpower. Patients do not experience the same compensatory hunger that typically defeats behavioral interventions alone.

What the Clinical Evidence Shows

The STEP (Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity) trial program, published primarily in the New England Journal of Medicine, provides the foundational evidence for semaglutide in weight management.

STEP 1 (Wilding et al., NEJM 2021) enrolled 1,961 adults with BMI ≥30, or ≥27 with at least one comorbidity, without type 2 diabetes. After 68 weeks of semaglutide 2.4mg weekly plus lifestyle counseling, participants lost an average of 14.9% of body weight — compared to 2.4% in the placebo group. More than 50% of participants in the semaglutide group lost at least 15% of body weight. No previously approved non-surgical weight loss medication had produced results of this magnitude.

STEP 2 examined adults with type 2 diabetes, where average weight loss was 9.6% with semaglutide 2.4mg — lower than in patients without diabetes, reflecting the additional metabolic complexity, but still clinically meaningful.

STEP 4 addressed a critical clinical question: what happens when patients stop semaglutide? Participants who discontinued the medication regained two-thirds of their lost weight within one year. This finding has direct implications for how physicians counsel patients — semaglutide treats obesity as a chronic condition requiring ongoing management, not a short-term course of medication.

The SELECT trial, published in 2023, extended the evidence beyond weight loss: semaglutide reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke, cardiovascular death) by 20% in overweight and obese adults without diabetes. This cardiovascular outcome data represents a significant development in obesity medicine and positions semaglutide as a treatment for metabolic disease broadly, not only a weight loss intervention.

How Semaglutide Is Used in a Physician-Supervised Program

Semaglutide 2.4mg is administered as a once-weekly subcutaneous injection, typically in the abdomen, thigh, or upper arm. Patients self-administer after instruction. The dose is not started at 2.4mg — a structured titration schedule is used to allow the gastrointestinal system to adapt and minimize side effects:

  • Weeks 1–4: 0.25mg weekly
  • Weeks 5–8: 0.5mg weekly
  • Weeks 9–12: 1.0mg weekly
  • Weeks 13–16: 1.7mg weekly
  • Week 17 onward: 2.4mg weekly (maintenance dose)

At Knoxville Weight Loss Clinic, physician oversight during this titration period is not optional — it is the clinical standard. Monthly monitoring allows the treating physician to assess weight loss progress, manage side effects, adjust dose timing if needed, and review lab results. Baseline labs typically include a metabolic panel, HbA1c, thyroid function, and lipid panel. These are not administrative formalities; they establish the clinical baseline required to prescribe safely and to evaluate response objectively.

Patients who cannot tolerate the 2.4mg maintenance dose may be maintained at 1.7mg — the prescribing information allows this, and clinical judgment governs the decision. Semaglutide does not work identically for every patient. Physician supervision enables individualized management that a prescription-only telehealth model cannot provide.

Who Qualifies and Important Contraindications

FDA-approved criteria for Wegovy are:

  • BMI ≥30 (obesity), or
  • BMI ≥27 (overweight) with at least one weight-related condition: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia

Semaglutide is contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN2). This contraindication derives from rodent carcinogenicity data; human risk has not been established, but the label requires exclusion of these patients as a precautionary measure. Semaglutide is also not indicated during pregnancy.

Patients with a history of pancreatitis, severe gastroparesis, or diabetic retinopathy requiring careful glycemic management require individualized evaluation before starting therapy. A complete medical history — not a brief online questionnaire — is the appropriate basis for this determination. Frequently Asked Questions

Is semaglutide the same as Ozempic?
Semaglutide is the active molecule in both Ozempic and Wegovy, but they are different FDA-approved products with different approved indications. Ozempic (semaglutide 0.5–2mg) is FDA-approved for type 2 diabetes; weight loss with Ozempic is an off-label use. Wegovy (semaglutide 2.4mg) is FDA-approved specifically for chronic weight management. The higher maintenance dose in Wegovy — 2.4mg versus Ozempic's maximum of 2mg — is what was studied and approved for weight loss, and it produces greater weight reduction than lower doses.
How much weight can I expect to lose on semaglutide?
In the STEP 1 trial (Wilding et al., NEJM 2021), participants using semaglutide 2.4mg lost an average of 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks. More than half of participants lost at least 15%, and approximately one-third lost 20% or more. Individual results vary based on starting weight, adherence, metabolic factors, and whether the patient has type 2 diabetes — STEP 2 showed an average 9.6% loss in patients with diabetes. These are population averages from controlled trials; your physician can discuss what range of response is realistic given your specific clinical profile.
How does semaglutide reduce appetite?
Semaglutide activates GLP-1 receptors in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus, the brain region that regulates energy intake and hunger signaling. It also slows gastric emptying, which prolongs the sense of fullness after meals. Together, these mechanisms reduce caloric intake by altering the physiological signals that drive hunger — not by suppressing appetite through stimulant effects, as older weight loss medications did. This is why patients often describe eating less without the struggle that characterizes behavioral calorie restriction alone.
How long do I need to take semaglutide?
Semaglutide treats obesity as a chronic metabolic disease, which means ongoing treatment is typically required to maintain results. The STEP 4 trial demonstrated that participants who discontinued semaglutide after achieving weight loss regained approximately two-thirds of that weight within one year. This is consistent with how other chronic diseases are managed — stopping treatment allows the underlying condition to return. Your physician will discuss long-term treatment planning, monitoring, and what discontinuation would mean for your individual situation.
Is compounded semaglutide the same as Wegovy?
No. Compounded semaglutide is not FDA-approved and has not undergone the manufacturing quality controls, stability testing, or clinical trials required for FDA approval. It was permitted under drug shortage provisions while branded semaglutide was in short supply, but the FDA has been restricting compounding as those shortages resolve. Potency, purity, and sterility of compounded products vary by compounding pharmacy. A physician-supervised program using FDA-approved Wegovy provides a different level of clinical accountability than sourcing compounded semaglutide outside of medical oversight.

Many commercial insurance plans and Medicare Advantage plans in Tennessee have added coverage for Wegovy, particularly following the SELECT cardiovascular outcomes data. However, prior authorization is typically required, with documentation of BMI criteria and comorbidities. What are the most common side effects of semaglutide?
The most common side effects are gastrointestinal: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, and abdominal discomfort. These occur most frequently during dose escalation and typically decrease or resolve as the body adjusts to the medication. The structured titration schedule — starting at 0.25mg and increasing over 16 weeks to the 2.4mg maintenance dose — is specifically designed to minimize these effects. Physician monitoring during titration allows dose adjustments if side effects are not tolerable at a given step. Serious adverse events, including pancreatitis and gallbladder disease, are rare but require physician awareness of warning signs.
